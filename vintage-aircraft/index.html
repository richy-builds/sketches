How to read a plate

Outline

A solid line is an edge you could see or touch on the real aircraft.

Hidden line

Short dashes mark structure behind a surface: a spar inside a wing, a wheel inside its bay.

Centreline

Long dash, short dash. The axis of symmetry, or the middle of a shaft or engine.

Dimension

Metres, measured tip to tip. The plates keep one scale across all three views.

Section

Hatching shows solid material where the drawing cuts through a part.

Checker's red

Red marks the feature each plate singles out for its detail drawing.

Wright Flyer

Experimental powered aeroplane, one pilot. United States, first flown 17 December 1903.

Wright Flyer, 1903
Crew1, prone
First flight17 December 1903, 12 seconds, 37 m
Span12.29 m
Length6.43 m
Height2.74 m
Wing area47.4 m²
EngineWright 4-cylinder inline, water-cooled, 12 hp
Speed48 km/h through the air
Best heightAbout 3 m
Weight274 kg empty, 341 kg with pilot
Built1

On the morning of 17 December 1903, at Kill Devil Hills in North Carolina, Orville Wright flew this machine 37 metres in twelve seconds into a headwind of more than 30 km/h. Three more flights followed, the last of 260 metres in 59 seconds, before a gust rolled the Flyer over on the sand and wrecked it. It never flew again.

Nothing about it was conventional. The elevator led and the rudders trailed, the pilot lay prone on the lower wing, and the propellers pushed. The brothers built the engine in their own shop when no manufacturer would supply one light enough, and carved the propellers as rotating wings from their own wind-tunnel data. What made it an aeroplane rather than a glider with a motor was control: the wings twisted along their span to bank, and the rudder moved with them to counter the yaw that twisting produced.

Sopwith F.1 Camel

Single-seat fighting scout. Great Britain, first flown 22 December 1916.

Sopwith F.1 Camel
Crew1
First flight22 December 1916
Span8.53 m
Length5.72 m
Height2.59 m
Wing area21.46 m²
EngineClerget 9B, 9-cylinder air-cooled rotary, 130 hp
Maximum speed185 km/h at 1,980 m
Service ceiling5,790 m
Armament2 × 7.7 mm Vickers machine guns
Built5,490

The Camel was the first British fighter to carry two synchronised machine guns, and the fairing over their breeches gave the aircraft its name. Nearly everything heavy sat within the front two metres: engine, guns, fuel and pilot. The Clerget rotary spun its whole crankcase and cylinders with the propeller, and the gyroscopic force of that spinning mass made the Camel turn right with startling speed while fighting its pilot through a left turn.

That temperament killed many novices in training and made experienced pilots nearly untouchable. Camels were credited with more enemy aircraft than any other Allied type of the war. This one is B6313, in which William Barker of 28 and later 66 Squadron scored forty-six victories, more than any other single airframe.

Douglas DC-3

Twin-engined airliner, 21 passengers. United States, first flown 17 December 1935.

Douglas DC-3
Crew2 or 3, plus 21 passengers
First flight17 December 1935
Span28.96 m
Length19.66 m
Height5.16 m
Wing area91.7 m²
Engines2 × Wright R-1820 Cyclone, 9-cylinder radials, 1,100 hp each
Maximum speed370 km/h at 2,590 m
Service ceiling7,070 m
Range2,400 km
Built607 civil; about 16,000 with military and licence versions

The DC-3 began as a request from American Airlines for a sleeper version of the DC-2, and Douglas widened the fuselage enough for fourteen berths. In the day layout it carried twenty-one passengers, and that turned out to be the number at which an airline could make money on passengers alone, without a mail contract. Within three years it was carrying most of the air traffic in the United States.

The airframe was built to last. Its wing is a multi-cellular structure of many small spars and stiffeners that tolerates damage and fatigue, and the stressed-skin fuselage shrugs off hard landings. Around sixteen thousand were built, including the wartime C-47, and a few hundred still fly. This aircraft is NC17334, Flagship Detroit, delivered in 1937 and still airworthy.

Supermarine Spitfire Mk I

Single-seat fighter. Great Britain, first flown 5 March 1936.

Supermarine Type 300, Mk I
Crew1
First flight5 March 1936
Span11.23 m
Length9.12 m
Height3.86 m
Wing area22.48 m²
EngineRolls-Royce Merlin II, liquid-cooled V-12, 1,030 hp
Maximum speed582 km/h at 5,700 m
Service ceiling10,400 m
Armament8 × 7.7 mm Browning machine guns
Mk I built1,567

Reginald Mitchell's Spitfire flew from Eastleigh on 5 March 1936 and looked unlike anything the Royal Air Force had ordered before. It had a thin elliptical wing, a monocoque fuselage of flush-riveted stressed skin, and a Rolls-Royce Merlin under a cowling with barely a hand's width to spare. The Mk I spread eight Browning machine guns across the wing, far enough outboard that the wing could stay thin at the root.

The wing that made it famous also made it slow to build. Its compound curves and single-spar structure took roughly two and a half times the labour of the Hurricane's. The aircraft drawn here wears the pre-war finish of 19 Squadron at Duxford, the first unit to receive the type, in August 1938.

Lockheed L-049 Constellation

Four-engined pressurised airliner. United States, first flown 9 January 1943.

Lockheed L-049 Constellation
Crew4, plus 44 to 81 passengers
First flight9 January 1943, as the C-69
Span37.49 m
Length29.0 m
Height7.21 m
Wing area153.3 m²
Engines4 × Wright R-3350 Duplex-Cyclone, 18-cylinder radials, 2,200 hp each
Maximum speed531 km/h at 5,800 m
Service ceiling7,710 m
Range3,700 km
Built88 L-049 and 22 C-69; 856 Constellations of all versions

Lockheed designed the Constellation for TWA and Howard Hughes, who wanted an airliner that could cross the United States without stopping, carrying forty passengers above the weather. The pressurised fuselage follows a continuous curve from nose to tail so that no frame is quite the same as its neighbour. The wing is the P-38 Lightning's, scaled up, and the four Wright Duplex-Cyclones were the most powerful engines on any airliner when it first flew.

The Army took the first aircraft as C-69 transports in 1943, and the airlines waited until 1946 for theirs. The triple tail was a concession to hangar doors, and it became the most recognisable silhouette of the piston age. This aircraft is NC86511 of TWA, delivered in 1946.
